*Summer is here, friends. I’ve returned from a few trips to find my garden overgrown. The maintenance of which has slowed my writing, but that doesn’t bother me as long as I have soil beneath my fingernails. I hope the change in season has been a welcome one for you. Meanwhile, back to the work.*

*-Nick*

---

In school, we learn the classics. Our elders proselytize about the classics. Some of us try, but in our feeble youth we struggle to understand the classics. That struggle sticks with us. It does not make us feel very good, and so we grow up and look for any opportunity to spurn the classics. To mock them. To ignore them. We do this at our own expense.

The more time I spend on this planet, surrounded by all these people, the more I realize that we have not internalized the classics well enough to overcome them and build something better than them. Look around and tell me if you see different.

We see people hoarding wealth as if they did not understand Dickens. We see people drifting through life detached from reality as if they never understood Camus. We see people perpetuate a cycle of shame and guilt as if they never understood Hawthorne. We can find more examples everywhere we look. We were taught the classics because they had something to teach us, and it is clear that we did not pay enough attention.

It has proven much easier to create an industry focused on avoiding the classics. Some choose to call them problematic while others call them woke. We foolishly ask “What could these dead people know about modern life?” So, we sell modern stories to talk about modern life and the publishing houses love the economic opportunity in that. But increasingly, these stories are no longer built on a world where the classics mattered. We move forward on weak foundations.

We were not taught the classics because they are holy scripture. We were taught the classics in order to surpass them. Previous generations understood this better than we do. We should not care if some of us hope to surpass them morally, while others hope to do so rhetorically. Hell, maybe we could simply hope to create something more interesting. It doesn’t matter how we surpass them, only that we try. That trying is impossible if we do not first understand what has come before.

That is certainly what I hope to do in my writing. But I reject the tantalizing shortcut that comes from delegitimizing the classics in order to say that surpassing them has no value. Or that I have surpassed them already. I want to overcome them while they maintain their legitimacy. For that is the greater challenge, and that holds the greater reward.
